A working call is not proof of a secure call.

During a compliance-driven review of a production contact-center environment, I investigated whether the connection between our Linux telephony server and the voice carrier protected both call signaling and media. Calls were completing. The encryption controls were not.

I designed a separate outbound connection using SIP over TLS and Secure RTP (SRTP), worked through integration failures in VICIdial and Asterisk, and collected multiple forms of evidence that the tested call was encrypted. After that successful pilot, the secure trunk was rolled out for live production calling. The objective was to protect a defined carrier connection without disrupting service—not to declare the entire phone system compliant.

Project at a glance

CHALLENGE

Plaintext carrier signaling and media despite healthy call completion.

APPROACH

Isolated PJSIP secure trunk, verified certificate trust, mandatory SRTP, application-owned configuration.

RESULT

Encrypted call validated by Asterisk and Twilio; the secure trunk subsequently deployed for production outbound traffic.

01 / THE BASELINE

The carrier was reachable. The traffic was not protected.

The initial read-only inventory found two active Twilio peers using legacy chan_sip over UDP port 5060. Their status was healthy, but both returned Encryption: No. The SIP TLS listener was disabled. A separate PJSIP listener used UDP port 5061—a useful reminder that port 5061 alone does not mean TLS is active.

SRTP support was already installed and loaded on the host. That did not change the baseline: the production carrier peers were not negotiating encrypted media. The problem existed in the actual transport and endpoint configuration, not merely in missing software.

02 / ENGINEERING CONSTRAINTS

The safest change was not an in-place change.

I owned the Linux-side investigation and implementation: platform and network discovery, Twilio trust validation, Asterisk configuration, VICIdial carrier integration, troubleshooting, controlled test execution, and evidence review. The design had to account for how the environment actually operated.

  • Shared production dependencies. Existing carriers were configured across telephony servers. Editing those shared records directly would have expanded the blast radius.
  • Generated configuration. VICIdial generated the Asterisk carrier files. A quick edit to the generated pjsip-vicidial.conf would eventually be overwritten.
  • Separate security and functionality criteria. A secure dial string that never connects is not an acceptable migration. A connected call that negotiates plaintext audio is not an acceptable remediation.

I chose a parallel, server-scoped secure carrier rather than replacing the shared production route during initial testing. The old routes were preserved as an operational recovery path; they were not configured as an automatic plaintext fallback from the new encrypted endpoint.

03 / DISCOVERY

Prove the network trust path before placing a call.

The host ran openSUSE Leap 15.6 with Asterisk 18.26.4-vici. PJSIP and SRTP functionality were present. Asterisk was controlled by vicidial.service; a separate asterisk.service existed but was disabled. I also checked the certificate and private key pairing, process access to the required files, address and port conflicts, and the planned transport objects.

Before touching campaign routing, I tested the assigned Twilio termination hostname with chain verification, hostname matching, and SNI enabled. All four resolved IPv4 gateways completed TLS 1.2 handshakes with trusted certificates. The diagnostic probes negotiated ECDHE-RSA-AES128-GCM-SHA256—a property of the preflight probes, not an independently captured cipher from the later Asterisk call.

That sequencing proved valuable: when Asterisk later produced a DNS-related error, I already knew the operating system could resolve the destination and establish a certificate-verified TLS connection.

04 / SECURE DESIGN

Two channels. Two encryption requirements.

Signaling and audio are separate problems. SIP sets up and controls the call; RTP carries voice packets. Moving SIP onto TLS without changing media would still leave the audio unprotected, so the new design explicitly required both controls.

On Twilio I provisioned a separate secure outbound trunk with IP-based authorization limited to a single-host /32 ACL. On Asterisk I explicitly selected its TLS transport and used media_encryption=sdes with media_encryption_optimistic=no. I kept the persistent transport, endpoint, and address-of-record definitions in the VICIdial carrier configuration rather than modifying generated output files.

05 / TROUBLESHOOTING

The implementation exposed two application-layer failures.

1. VICIdial rewrote a legitimate-looking SIP contact.

The first attempt included the URI parameter ;transport=tls at the end of the carrier contact. After VICIdial persisted the record, the contact appeared as :5061transport=tls. The removed semicolon left a malformed address and an unavailable endpoint.

The transformation was consistent with semicolon sanitization identified in the VICIdial source review. Instead of bypassing the application by editing generated Asterisk files, I corrected the persistent contact to a semicolon-free hostname and port, and retained the endpoint's explicit TLS transport. Source review showed that the selected PJSIP transport could still choose TLS without the URI parameter.

2. DNS was working; Asterisk's new transport state was not fully ready.

After correcting the contact, Asterisk still reported PJLIB_UTIL_EDNSNOANSWERREC. I compared that error with working system DNS, successful certificate-verified TLS probes, and the Asterisk transport configuration. The evidence did not support changing resolvers, disabling certificate checks, or pinning a single carrier IP.

The version-specific source review suggested that PJSIP's resolver had captured transport availability at process startup. A reload had introduced the TLS listener, but the resolver still behaved as if that transport type were unavailable. This remained a source-supported diagnosis, rather than an instrumented proof of every internal state transition.

After inspecting the installed vicidial.service unit and its launcher, confirming no active calls at the maintenance snapshot, and performing a controlled VICIdial-managed restart, the remote contact qualified successfully. The loaded TLS settings remained strict. I did not enable a second Asterisk service or downgrade any security requirements.

I then compared the generated configuration with the live PJSIP objects. The corrected settings survived the configuration lifecycle; the requested rebuild flag was confirmed returned to its idle value. The encrypted endpoint's availability was established before separately evaluating campaign routing.

06 / VALIDATION

Proof required more than one green indicator.

I designed the controlled acceptance test to answer four separate questions: Could Linux establish a trusted TLS path? Did Asterisk load the intended security policy? Was SRTP actually negotiated on an answered call? Did the carrier independently record the call as secure?

The initial echo test placed one authorized call with no automatic retries, a 30-second answer timeout, and a 120-second absolute bound. The test was designed not to alter configuration, enable recording, or capture sensitive SDP/SDES key material. I answered the call, heard my speech echoed, and used # to terminate it. This verified functional two-way audio and DTMF behavior for that tested path.

I correlated the test with the matching Twilio export. The carrier reported a completed 45-second call with Secure Trunking=true. I preserved the exported duration rather than recalculating it from rounded timestamps. A trunk identifier was null in that export, so the local endpoint configuration and matching call details were used for route attribution without inventing missing metadata.

These results supported one defensible conclusion: the tested outbound Linux-to-Twilio leg used verified TLS signaling and negotiated secure media. This verification was the acceptance milestone preceding production rollout. The pilot itself did not establish security for other call legs or stored recordings.

07 / PRODUCTION ROLLOUT

From isolated test call to live production traffic.

With the dedicated trunk configured, qualified, and proven through an answered TLS/SRTP pilot, I proceeded with a controlled rollout into the production calling environment. As of October 13, 2026, the secure trunk is in production and being used for outbound calls. That deployment status is confirmed by the environment owner; the precise cutover time, campaign coverage, and total migrated call volume are not independently established by the sanitized material presented here.

An October 13 follow-up review recorded 19 Twilio call entries marked Secure Trunking=true: nine completed, six no-answer, and four failed. Seven calls were correlated with the supplied Asterisk output. These are historical findings retained from the earlier analysis; the raw October 13 attachments are not included or independently re-parsed in this public package.

These call outcomes provide useful operational follow-up to the rollout, but nineteen secure-marked entries do not mean nineteen successful encrypted audio sessions. The failed and unanswered attempts were not treated as completed-media evidence, and this sample does not establish that every campaign or call leg migrated—or a long-term call-completion rate.

The review also found local FastAGI connection refusals—a separate logging/application dependency that could coexist with a successful encrypted carrier connection. I did not misdiagnose those refusals as TLS failures, or claim to have repaired them in this project. Conference and recording-related events in the call flow were not proof of recording integrity or encryption at rest.

08 / RESULT + ENGINEERING LESSONS

A measured security improvement, with its boundary intact.

The secure trunk was successfully deployed and rolled out for production outbound calling. What began as a discovery of plaintext signaling and media progressed through a dedicated PJSIP design, certificate and transport validation, two integration repairs, a verified encrypted call, and finally operational use. This was achieved without a full telephony-platform replacement or Asterisk rebuild. Shared legacy configurations were kept unchanged during staging; I do not assume that all older routes were subsequently retired.

Production use does not close every assurance item. Certificate renewal and live TLS refresh, call-setup capacity under sustained load, full campaign coverage, the FastAGI refusal, transfer/hold/failover behavior, and independent negative testing against plaintext media still require separate evidence or follow-up. Inbound, agent-side, inter-server, PSTN, and recording-at-rest protection were outside this case study’s validation. I do not represent the rollout as organization-wide encryption or HIPAA compliance.

Configuration expresses intent. Runtime negotiation and corroborating evidence establish what actually happened.

That is the engineering practice this case study demonstrates: start with the actual baseline, isolate the change, understand the owning service and application, challenge misleading error messages, and prove outcomes without overstating them.
